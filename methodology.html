Methodology

Scope & Institutions

The tracker covers about 1,900 active four-year public, private nonprofit and private for-profit institutions across the 50 states and D.C. that primarily confer a bachelor’s degree or above, as defined by the U.S. Department of Education. Specifically, we are collecting and analyzing the last decade of financial and demographic data from the National Center for Education Statistics’ Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System (IPEDS). We only include institutions flagged as active in the most recent year in the IPEDS system (as of our launch: 2024). We also exclude colleges that provide all programs through distance education. We limit the tracker to institutions that IPEDS classifies as “Degree-granting, primarily baccalaureate or above.” That category refers to institutions where the total number of degrees and certificates awarded at the bachelor’s level or higher is more than 50 percent of the total number of degrees and certificates awarded.

We refresh the IPEDS Complete Data Files three times a year when the federal government publishes new data files in the fall, winter and spring. Definitions of IPEDS variables can be found here.

Limitations

Our enrollment, financial and outcomes data lags because data releases from the U.S. Department of Education lag by one to two academic years. Because we update the college cuts, accreditation actions and federal research funding cuts each week, this tracker may not show the most recent data.

The tracker does not provide a report card-style grade for an institution. And it does not directly state whether an institution is likely to close or roll back offerings. Instead, we show trends in data that can highlight whether an institution is showing multiple concerning trends: for example, a college with one or two red indicators may be responsibly offering more financial aid to students while buttressed by a strong endowment and increased revenues. Conversely, a college with eight years of losses in the last decade and declining enrollment and net tuition revenue may be facing significant financial challenges.

Geography and outcomes

Data about a college’s geography – city, state and how urban the local area is – comes from IPEDS. IPEDS has four categories for how urban or rural a college’s surroundings are: city, suburb, town and rural. It also has breakdowns for large, midsize, small, fringe, distant and remote. A town or rural community may be classified as fringe, distant and remote depending on how far away it is from a community of at least 50,000 people. Definitions of fringe, distant and remote are available here. For example: for towns, fringe means the college is up to 10 miles from a community of at least 50,000 people. A college gets labeled as distant if it’s between 10 and up to 35 miles away. Colleges that are more than 35 miles away are classified as remote.

Data about graduation rates comes from IPEDS. The tracker shows the percentage of students who graduate within six years – a rate long used as the benchmark for graduation rates by colleges, researchers and lawmakers despite some pushback from critics who question whether the benchmark should be four years.

Debt and earnings data come from the Department of Education’s College Scorecard data. We show median earnings for employed students who received federal aid 10 years after entering college. We also show the median total federal loan debt for students who graduated.

For data about how much graduate students took out in Grad PLUS loans, we use the average Grad PLUS disbursement per recipient from the U.S. Department of Education’s Federal Student Aid Direct Loan dashboard.

Financial Indicators

Revenue, expenses, net tuition per full-time equivalent student, endowment, spending, research expenses and revenue from state and federal grants and contracts come from IPEDS Finance surveys. Dollar figures are adjusted to 2024 dollars using the Consumer Price Index (CPI-U) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. We calculate the discount rate for private nonprofit colleges by dividing unfunded institutional grants by gross tuition revenue. Unfunded means that the grant money comes from unrestricted institution funds.

The federal Financial Responsibility Composite Score comes from filings published annually by the U.S. Department of Education’s office of Federal Student Aid (FSA). The last update of those scores was for the 2022-2023 year. The federal government calculates these scores on a range from -1 to 3 by looking at three ratios designed to gauge the financial health of an institution: primary reserve ratio, equity ratio and net income ratio. A score of 1.5 means an institution passes. Leading researchers have pointed out that even colleges that end up closing due to financial challenges can achieve a passing score. Still, the scores can be a sign that the government is scrutinizing a college’s financial health. Colleges get placed into an oversight zone if they score between 1.0 and 1.4, and are subject to more financial monitoring. Colleges with scores at 0.9 and below have to submit a letter of credit to keep getting federal financial aid.

The tracker also displays, if applicable, whether an institution is on the highest level of federal cash monitoring. The federal government can delay federal financial aid to colleges with serious accreditation, financial or administrative concerns. It can do so by placing them on what’s known as heightened cash monitoring level 2. The tracker shows if an institution has been on heightened cash monitoring at any point since December 2024 and for what reason (for example: financial responsibility or adverse action taken by an accreditor).

On a college’s individual institutional profile, the tracker may show blue, red or yellow fields depending on whether a specific financial or enrollment trend is getting better or worse. For five-year change metrics, declines of 5 percent or more appear red, increases of 5 percent or more appear blue, and changes in between appear yellow. For example, a college that has seen a 25 percent decrease in revenue from 2019 to 2024 will show red. A college with a 1 percent difference will show yellow. A college with a 15 percent increase will show blue. The exception is the tuition discount rate trend, which is reversed: increases appear red because that can signal more financial pressure.

Other indicators show red for a “yes” response and blue for a “no” response, including whether the college ended the latest year at a loss and whether it has been in the red or seen a decline in enrollment in three of the last five years. A loss is a year in which expenses exceed revenue. Colleges that ended the year with zero or one losses in the past decade show blue. The tracker shows yellow for two to four losses and red for five or more. For the federal financial responsibility score, colleges with a score of 1.5 or above show blue. Scores below 1.5 show red.

Enrollment & Staffing

For enrollment data, we rely on the IPEDS 12-month unduplicated headcount, which includes both full-time and part-time students. Our breakdown of international enrollment on the undergraduate and graduate level is from the IPEDS 12-month enrollment component, which has breakdowns for U.S. nonresident alien students. IPEDS defines those students as “individuals who are not citizens or nationals of the United States and who do not have the right to remain in the country indefinitely.”

Staffing data comes from the IPEDS Human Resources component, which reports the total headcount of part-time and full-time employees. “Instructional staff” is defined as employees whose major regular assignment is instruction, including those with time for research. Total staff includes both “instructional” and “non-instructional” employees.

We use the IPEDS Student-to-Faculty Ratio, which is calculated by dividing the number of fall full-time equivalent undergraduate students by the number of fall full-time equivalent instructional staff at the undergraduate level, excluding graduate and professional programs. IPEDS calculates full-time equivalent students by taking the number of full-time students and adding one-third of the number of part-time students. That formula is the same for staff.

College Cuts

Layoffs, program suspensions, teach-outs, and closures are pulled weekly from the CollegeCuts Tracker, supplemented by reporting from The Hechinger Report. We verify each entry from CollegeCuts weekly before adding it to our tracker and also add cuts we find separately.

Accreditation Actions

Accreditation actions are compiled from the six regional accreditors recognized by the U.S. Department of Education (Higher Learning Commission, Middle States Commission on Higher Education, New England Commission of Higher Education, Northwest Commission on Colleges and Universities, Southern Association of Colleges and Schools Commission on Colleges, Western Association of Schools and Colleges Senior College and University Commission). We include warnings, probations, notices of concern, show-cause actions, closures, teach-out approvals, and the removal or lifting of any of the above. Specialized accreditors, such as those overseeing art schools, are not currently covered.

The tracker pulls data weekly from the U.S. Department of Education Database of Accredited Postsecondary Institutions and Programs (DAPIP). The tracker also pulls data weekly from the six regional accreditor individual websites because DAPIP data can lag. The Hechinger Report verifies each entry before it appears on the website.

Research Funding Cuts

This tracker displays federal research funding cuts since the start of the second Trump administration in 2025. It uses data from the Grant Witness database, covering grants from the National Institutes of Health (NIH), National Science Foundation, Environmental Protection Agency, Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. We display grants that Grant Witness marks as terminated, as well as NIH grants that are marked frozen. Legal challenges remain active in some cases. Grants that have been reinstated or unfrozen are excluded. We exclude grants where the university is passing funds on to community groups by looking at project titles and abstracts and filtering phrases including “manage and distribute funds” and “regional grantmaker.” We only display grants with a remaining award amount of at least $100.

Bond Ratings

Each college profile includes a link to search for the institution on EMMA, the Electronic Municipal Market Access system operated by the Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board. EMMA is the official public repository for municipal bond disclosures, including rating agency reports from Moody’s, S&P, and Fitch. The Hechinger Report does not republish or license rating agency content; the EMMA link is a navigation aid so readers can find primary source material directly. Not all colleges have issued public debt or carry a bond rating: for-profit institutions and many smaller colleges are unrated, and a blank search result does not indicate financial distress.

Every institution page and landing table has a download button that produces a plain text file separated by commas containing data across all years. This tracker is free and available for the public.
